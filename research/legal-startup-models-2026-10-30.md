# Legal marketplace / directory / legal-AI business models: lessons for LegalGrads
Researched 2026-10-30 (PT). Only figures found in cited sources are included. Items marked [unverified] were not checked this pass.

## 1. Executive takeaways
- **Fee tied to a completed matter means trouble. A flat fee for advertising or leads is fine.** NJ's joint ethics opinion (2017) held Avvo's per-service "marketing fee" ($10 on a $39.95 call) was an impermissible referral fee and fee-split. The same opinion says lawyers MAY pay a flat amount for ads or leads that may not turn into clients.
- **Avvo Legal Services died from ethics opinions, not lack of demand.** 8 states (NY, OH, PA, SC, NJ, UT, VA, IN) told lawyers not to participate. Internet Brands shut it down in July 2018. The directory and ratings survived.
- **The NY opinion (1132) adds a second test: don't "vouch."** A satisfaction guarantee plus your own ratings plus taking a cut counts as paying for a recommendation (Rule 7.2(a)).
- **California is stricter on matching.** The Court of Appeal held LegalMatch was an unregistered lawyer referral service under B&P §6155 because it connected clients to lawyers for pay, even without judging the legal issues. Browse-only plus lawyer-paid flat advertising is the safe lane. Any "we match you" flow needs State Bar LRS certification.
- **UpCounsel (SMB-to-freelance-lawyer, the closest analog to LegalGrads)** raised $26M and was sued over fee-sharing, fake ratings, and fee misrepresentation (LegalForce v. UpCounsel; partly survived dismissal in 2019, then settled). It wound down in 2020 through a LinkedIn asset deal, then continued under new owners. Lesson: a take-rate on SMB legal work is thin, and it's a legal target.
- **Ratings are legally defensible but costly in goodwill.** Browne v. Avvo (2007) and Davis v. Avvo (2018) both lost on First Amendment opinion grounds, and the "Pro" badge was held to be puffery. Still, ratings on unclaimed profiles are the biggest source of lawyer backlash. Keeping no ratings is right.
- **Priori shows a compliant markup model.** The client pays a composite rate (lawyer's rate plus a Priori management fee), providers join free, and Priori says it is not a law firm or referral service. It is B2B and curated. This is the closest precedent to Ryan's "attorney keeps 100%, client pays a platform fee" idea, but it was not tested against CA §6155. It needs CA ethics counsel before launch.
- **Fiverr's structure, mapped to law:** buyers pay a 5.5% service fee (+$3.50 under $200) and sellers pay 20%. In law, the seller-side 20% is the forbidden part. A buyer-side fee is less clearly prohibited but is still a CA referral-service risk if the platform matches.
- **Thumbtack's model is the most law-compatible:** no subscription, and pros pay per lead at prices that vary by job. This matches NJ's "flat fee for leads" allowance. The fee is not tied to whether the lawyer gets hired.
- **Don't market AI as a lawyer.** In Feb 2025 the FTC finalized an order requiring DoNotPay to pay $193,000, notify 2021–23 subscribers, and stop claiming it performs like a lawyer without evidence.
- **Legal-AI money is in B2B SaaS sold to lawyers, not consumers.** Harvey reached an $8B valuation (Dec 2025), Legora $1.8B (Oct 2025), Spellbook a $50M Series B at $350M post-money, and EvenUp over $2B. That's a later upsell path: sell tools to claimed lawyers.
- **CA AB 931 (signed Oct 10, 2025, effective for contracts from Jan 1, 2026)** bars CA lawyers from sharing fees with out-of-state ABSs, plus a broader anti-"anything of value for referral" rule. It exempts fixed-dollar contracts that aren't tied to referrals or leads or contingent on recovery. This reinforces flat, non-referral pricing.

## 3. Recommended structure options (ranked)
1. **Directory + flat lawyer-paid advertising (Avvo/Justia lane). Best now.** Free browse, free claim, paid upgrade (featured placement clearly labeled as an ad, photo, booking link) at a fixed monthly price. Pros: matches the ethics-opinion safe harbor and needs no LRS certification. Cons: new lawyers have little budget, and it needs traffic first.
2. **Hybrid: directory + flat pay-per-inquiry (Thumbtack-style)**, where the lawyer pays a fixed price per contact no matter the outcome, and the consumer chooses the lawyer (no matching). Pros: pay-for-results feel, allowed in NJ's reading. Cons: CA §6155/LegalMatch risk grows the more the platform steers. Get a CA ethics opinion first.
3. **Marketplace with booking and a client-side platform fee (Priori/Fiverr buyer fee).** Pros: Ryan's "lawyer keeps 100%" pitch. Cons: the most regulatory exposure (referral service, and fee-split if the fee scales with the legal fee). Only consider it as a State Bar-certified LRS or after counsel approves. Avoid any seller-side percentage.
4. Later add-on: SaaS for claimed lawyers (intake, scheduling, AI drafting). Subscription revenue that isn't tied to referrals.

## 4. What NOT to copy
- Avvo Legal Services' per-service "marketing fee" or any percentage of the legal fee
- Satisfaction guarantees and refunds that vouch for lawyers (NY 1132)
- Algorithmic matching or "we'll find your lawyer" in CA without LRS certification (LegalMatch)
- Ratings or scores on unclaimed profiles, or any rating that pay can influence (Davis v. Avvo allegation; UpCounsel fake-ratings claim)
- Opaque fee disclosures (UpCounsel claim)
- "AI lawyer" or "robot lawyer" marketing (DoNotPay FTC order)
- Bundled consumer "legal plans" without registration (NJ on LegalZoom/Rocket Lawyer)
